Ranking Declining Content Pages: Observable Signals vs a Transparent Baseline Muhammad Laeeq ur Rehman · FlyRank Machine Learning Internship · October 2026 Abstract FlyRank tracks the search performance of content published for its clients; as pages age, some lose traffic, and review time is limited, so the practical problem is deciding which pages to review first. Can observable page signals rank pages that are observed to be declining better than a transparent baseline score? On an anonymized slice of 30,000 FlyRank content pages from 32 clients, we trained logistic regression, decision tree and random forest models and tested them on held-out clients (2,325 pages from 6 clients) against a weighted visibility, freshness and position score. The random forest ranked best, with 74% of its top 50 pages observed declining versus 24% for the baseline and about 39% for a random pick (ROC AUC 0.750 versus 0.627), though the model was selected on the same split and the top-50 sample is small. The results are directional decision support for ordering refresh reviews, not proof that refreshing a page improves its trend. 1 Introduction2 Data3 Methodology4 Results5 Limitations6 Recommendations7 Reproducibility8 Acknowledgments 1 Introduction The content problem. This study comes from a real problem in FlyRank’s content-performance data. FlyRank follows how published pages perform in search for its clients. Pages age, and a share of them show a falling search trend. A content team cannot review every page, so the practical question is which pages to look at first for a refresh review. The question. Can observable page signals (age, freshness, visibility, search position, content type) rank pages that are observed to be declining better than a transparent weighted score? A hand-written score fixes its weights in advance; a model can combine many signals and be tested on clients it has never seen. The answer is meant to support the order of refresh reviews, not to claim that a refresh causes recovery. 2 Data The data is the anonymized starter slice of the FlyRank ML Internship dataset: 30,000 content items from 32 clients and 44 columns, one row per pseudonymized page. It reports 90-day search and engagement metrics plus last-30 and previous-30-day comparisons. It has no calendar date column, so validation is by client, not by date. Client names, domains, URLs, titles and queries were removed before release. Excluded as features: hashed content and client IDs (used only for grouping); trend fields (trend_direction, trend_pct), because the label is derived from them; and last-30 and previous-30-day comparison columns, to avoid indirect leakage into the label. No rows were dropped (30,000 raw, 30,000 prepared; no duplicate page IDs). The columns with the most missing values are the content provider (71.5%) and word and character counts (25.7%). 3 Methodology Assumptions and label A page whose observed 90-day trend direction is “down” is called declining (trend_direction == 'down'). 54.2% of pages are positive. The slice is treated as representative enough for a first, directional analysis. Features 26 model inputs: 18 numeric (visibility, clicks and sessions on log scale, active days, age, days since update, CTR, average position, engagement, scroll, AI traffic share, word and character counts, search volume, competition, CPC) and 8 categorical (competition level, content type, intent, and age, freshness, word-count, impression and position tiers). One-hot encoding gives 52 columns. Baseline A transparent weighted score: 40% visibility (percentile of log 90-day impressions), 30% freshness risk (percentile of days since last update), 25% position opportunity (closeness to the top positions, weighted by visibility) and 5% depth gap (thin content weighted by visibility). Pages are ranked highest score first. The score does not use the label or any trend field. Validation design A client-holdout split with seed 42: 27,675 training pages and 2,325 test pages from 6 clients the models never saw. Logistic regression, a decision tree and a random forest were compared with the baseline on the same test pages. Leakage checks Trend fields and last-30 and previous-30-day columns are excluded from features, hashed IDs are used only for grouping, and a code check in the notebook confirms that none of these columns is among the 26 model features. 4 Results Because the use is a review queue, the main metric is precision at top k: the share of the k highest-ranked pages that are observed declining. The label is an observed downward trend. This work does not show that refreshing a page causes recovery. Model chosen on the reporting split. The best model was picked by precision@50 on the same held-out clients used to report results, so its score is somewhat optimistic. Small top-k. Precision@50 rests on 50 pages (roughly ±0.06 noise). Small, anonymized slice. 30,000 pages from 32 clients; the test split is 2,325 pages from only 6 clients. Results are directional, not production evidence. Client effects. The test clients have a lower declining rate (about 39%) than the full data (54%). Errors may cluster by client, and new clients may behave differently. Feature importance is association. Importances come from a model refit on all 30,000 pages; their direction was not tested. The refresh queue is illustrative. Queue scores for all pages come from models refit on all rows, so scores for training clients are in-sample. The queue shows the workflow, not a performance estimate. Reason codes are descriptive. Some codes (for example “declining with demand”) are derived from the observed label and are not model findings. Label and time. “Declining” depends on how trend direction was computed upstream, and the slice has no calendar dates, so time-based validation was not possible. 6 Ranked recommendations Ordered by how well the evidence supports them. All are decision-support, not causal claims. Order the first review batch with the model ranking, not the baseline score. The forest’s top 50 was 74% observed declining against 24% for the baseline and about 39% for a random pick. Confidence: medium, because the model was selected on the same split and the top-50 sample is small. Retire the baseline score as an ordering rule. Its top 20, 50 and 100 were observed declining less often than a random pick (0.15, 0.24, 0.36 against about 0.39); ROC AUC 0.627. Confidence: medium. Look first at visible pages: many days with impressions and high 90-day impressions. These are the two strongest model signals. Confidence: medium-low; importance shows what the model used, not direction or cause. Check pages with weaker search position and older content next. These are the next strongest signals. Confidence: low; direction was not tested, so inspect a sample before acting. Re-test on new clients and a later time window before using the ranking operationally. The evidence rests on 32 clients, 6 test clients, one slice, no dates and a model chosen on the reporting split.
